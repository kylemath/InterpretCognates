1. Introduction & Theoretical Background Paper §1–§2

Do neural machine translation models learn language-universal conceptual structure, or do they primarily organize representations around language identity and surface-form similarity? This question sits at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability in natural language processing and a classic debate in cognitive science over whether multilingual speakers access a language-independent conceptual store or rely on language-specific lexical networks (Kroll & Stewart, 1994; Dijkstra & van Heuven, 2002; Correia et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2025).

In NLP interpretability, Chang, Tu, and Bergen (2022) demonstrated that multilingual masked language model embeddings can be partitioned into language-sensitive axes (high variance, low effective dimensionality) and language-neutral axes (lower variance, higher effective dimensionality). In cognitive neuroscience, multivariate fMRI decoding shows that while the core left-lateralized fronto-temporal language network processes lexical and syntactic structure across native languages (Malik-Moraleda et al., 2022), the bilateral anterior temporal lobe (ATL) acts as an amodal conceptual hub supporting cross-lingual semantic equivalence (Correia et al., 2014; Lambon Ralph et al., 2017), and bilingual cortex exhibits two-level language tuning combining a global language-indicator shift with semantic-cluster-specific tuning modulations (Chen et al., 2025).

We probe the 600-million-parameter distilled dense checkpoint of Meta's NLLB-200 translation model (facebook/nllb-200-distilled-600M, 12 encoder layers, d = 1024; NLLB Team et al., 2022) across 135 typologically diverse languages and 101 Swadesh core concepts, contrasting target words embedded in a shared English carrier frame against decontextualized native bare-word inputs. Specifically, we test whether NLLB-200's encoder space decomposes into a grand mean ē, a shared conceptual manifold μ̂c, an additive language shift ℓ̂, and a structured concept-by-language interaction residual ε̂c,ℓ (Paper Equation 1):

Paper Equation 1 — Two-Way Additive Representation Decomposition

ec,ℓ  =  ē  +  μ̂c  +  ℓ̂  +  ε̂c,ℓ  (Σc μ̂c = 0,  Σℓ ℓ̂ = 0)

We organize our investigation into six progressive empirical studies that match Paper Section 4:

  1. Additive Language-Shift Geometry & Layer-Wise Emergence (§3.1 / Paper §4.1): Two-way variance partitioning, eigenvalue Participation Ratio (PR) dimensionality analysis, the Conceptual Store Metric (CSM) across bare-word, carrier-frame, and cross-script regimes, and layer-wise emergence across Encoder Layers 0–11.
  2. Swadesh Convergence Hierarchy, Isotropy & Carrier Baseline (§3.2 / Paper §4.2): Cross-lingual convergence across eight semantic domains, ABTT isotropy hyperparameter sensitivity (k ∈ {0, 1, 3, 5, 10}), and carrier-frame vs. bare-word rank invariance.
  3. Surface-Form Confounds & Zipfian Subword Compactness (§3.3 / Paper §4.3): Orthographic and phonological Levenshtein regressions across 92 Latin-script languages, disentangling modern international loanwords from POS-matched and strictly tangible concrete nouns, and isolating Zipfian subword tokenization compactness via ANCOVA.
  4. Genealogical Structure in Language-Pair Distances (§3.4 / Paper §4.4): Stratified Mantel tests against ASJP v20 phonetic distances (both standard NLD and Jäger's 2018 off-diagonal normalized LDND), within-subfamily block permutations, cross-script subfamily tests, and Revised Hierarchical Model (RHM) training-resource asymmetry.
  5. Universal Colexification Structure (§3.5 / Paper §4.5): Evaluating all 5,050 Swadesh concept pairs against CLICS³ cross-family colexification frequency, separating single-family accidental homophony from recurrent cross-family polysemy across string-distinct, cognate-excluded, and within-category controls.
  6. Domain-Level Geometry: Berlin & Kay Color Hierarchy & Relational Offsets (§3.6 / Paper §4.6): Recovering the Berlin & Kay (1969) evolutionary color hierarchy on PC1 across layers and regimes, and testing second-order relational offset vectors using distance-controlled residuals and doubly-centered perturbation coupling cos(ε̂a,ℓ, ε̂b,ℓ).

2. Materials, Methods & Single-Concept Intuition Paper §3 · Fig. 1

We analyze facebook/nllb-200-distilled-600M, the dense 600M-parameter checkpoint distilled from NLLB-200's 54.5B Mixture-of-Experts translation model (NLLB Team et al., 2022). Its shared 12-layer Transformer encoder (hidden dimension d = 1024, 16 attention heads, 256,000-token SentencePiece vocabulary) maps all source languages into a common representational space before target-language generation is conditioned on a decoder prefix token.

Stimulus Corpora & Outlier Quality Filtering (Paper §3.1)

Our primary lexical corpus comprises 101 core concepts from the Swadesh list (Swadesh, 1952) translated across 141 candidate NLLB-200 languages. Applying an automated, non-subjective quality filter (excluding 6 languages whose mean embedding distance exceeded 3× the interquartile fence above Q3 due to script mismatch or archaic literary glosses: ary_Arab, bod_Tibt, uzb_Latn, knc_Latn, san_Deva, and ban_Latn) yields 135 languages spanning 19 language families and 24 writing systems (13,635 concept–language pairs). We complement the Swadesh list with two 60-item non-Swadesh benchmarks (an uncurated modern loanword set and a POS-matched non-loanword concrete noun set) and the 11 basic color terms of Berlin and Kay (1969).

Dual Embedding Extraction Regimes & Isotropy Correction (Paper §3.2)

  1. Shared Carrier-Frame Regime: Each target-language word is spliced into a uniform English grammatical slot — "I saw a {word} near the river." (or "I drink {word}." for the single-concept illustration below) — with the SentencePiece source-language prefix set to the target language (tokenizer.src_lang = lang_code). Subword hidden states matching the exact character span of {word} are isolated and mean-pooled, achieving 13,635 / 13,635 (100.0%) exact target-span alignment with zero full-sentence fallbacks.
  2. Decontextualized Bare-Word Regime: Each native word is encoded in complete isolation with its native language prefix and zero surrounding English context tokens, isolating intrinsic lexical representations prior to contextual self-attention mixing.
  3. All-But-The-Top (ABTT) Isotropy Correction: Following Mu and Viswanath (2018) and Rajaee and Pilehvar (2022), we subtract the global mean and project out the top k = 3 dominant principal components (validating stability across k ∈ {0, 1, 3, 5, 10}; Paper Equation 4).

3.1 Additive Language-Shift Geometry & Layer-Wise Emergence Paper §4.1 · Exps 1a–1c · Figs. 2–4

We first test our central structural hypothesis (Paper Equation 1): that NLLB-200's encoder representations decompose into a shared conceptual centroid μ̂c plus an additive per-language shift ℓ̂. Applying exact two-way sum-of-squares partitioning across all 101 × 135 = 13,635 embeddings shows how contextual framing redistributes variance between language identity and concept identity:

  • Decontextualized Bare-Word Regime: Concept centroids μ̂c account for 44.7% of total centered variance, per-language shifts ℓ̂ account for 25.7%, and the concept-by-language interaction residual ε̂c,ℓ accounts for 29.6%.
  • Shared Carrier-Frame Regime ("I saw a {word} near the river."): Self-attention with the shared context tokens reduces per-language shift variance nearly sevenfold to 3.8%, while concept centroids expand to 64.4% and the interaction residual remains stable at 31.8%.
  • Participation Ratio (PR) Dimensionality Asymmetry (generalizing Chang et al., 2022): Eigendecomposition of the doubly-centered distance matrices reveals that the language-shift subspace {ℓ̂} is concentrated in a low-dimensional manifold with participation ratio PR = (Σλi)² / Σλi² = 13.3 on bare words (66.6% of variance in the top 10 PCs; PR = 18.8 in the carrier frame), whereas the conceptual manifold {μ̂c} spans nearly three times the effective dimensionality (PR = 38.7, with the top 10 PCs capturing only 40.1% of variance).
Experiments 1a & 1b — Conceptual Store Metric (Paper Figure 2)

Compared against a concept-permutation null (CSMnull = 1.01 ± 0.0004), subtracting per-language mean vectors ℓ̂ increases the between-concept to within-concept cosine distance ratio (CSM) on decontextualized native bare words from 1.15 to 2.46 (a 2.14× improvement; vs. 1.76 under simple global mean-centering), and across strictly disjoint scripts from 1.15 to 2.60 (2.27×). In the shared carrier frame, raw CSM starts higher at 2.25 and rises to 2.69 (1.19×; cross-script 2.47 → 2.94, 1.19×) after per-language centering, showing that both regimes converge onto the same underlying conceptual store once language offsets are removed.

Layer-Wise Emergence & POS-Selective Terminal Unpacking Across Encoder Blocks 0–11 Paper §4.1 · Exp 1c · Fig. 3

To trace how this shared conceptual manifold is constructed across depth, we evaluate both Swadesh convergence and CSM at every encoder layer l ∈ {0, …, 11} across our 39-language typologically balanced subset (Paper Figure 3, fig_layerwise_trajectory.pdf). Cross-lingual convergence rises sharply from 0.35 at Layer 0 (largest single-layer jump at Layer 1) to reach peak cross-lingual alignment at Layer 10 (μ = 0.86), before dipping slightly to 0.80 at Layer 11 as representations prepare for decoder cross-attention.

Why does raw convergence dip slightly from Layer 10 (0.86) to Layer 11 (0.80) while language-centered CSM continues rising from 3.89 (Layer 10) to its global peak of 4.01 at Layer 11 (after its steepest mid-network inflection at Layer 6)? Decomposing the CSM numerator and denominator reveals the exact geometric mechanism (Voita et al., 2019, 2021):

  • Layers 6 → 10 (Within-Concept Contraction): Raw between-concept centroid cosine distance stays virtually constant (0.466 → 0.462) while within-concept cross-lingual dispersion contracts by 43.3% (0.240 → 0.136), driving centered CSM to 3.89.
  • Layer 10 → 11 (Anisotropic Cone Expansion & POS-Selective Unpacking): From Layer 10 to Layer 11, within-concept dispersion expands by +45.5% (0.136 → 0.198), but between-concept centroid separation expands even faster by +47.1% (0.462 → 0.680) as the final LayerNorm and FFN open the representation cone for decoder cross-attention, lifting centered CSM to 4.01. Crucially, concrete nouns fitting the carrier template's nominal slot (rain, tree, dog, fish, bird, cloud, star) do not dip at all at Layer 11 (peaking at C(c) = 0.95–0.96); the late-layer divergence is driven selectively by grammatical Pronouns (Δ = −0.15) and Actions/verbs (Δ = −0.09) unpacking target-language morphosyntactic features (person, number, valency) for decoding.

3.2 Swadesh Convergence Hierarchy, Isotropy Robustness & Carrier-Frame Baseline Paper §4.2 · Exps 2a–2c · Figs. 5–8

Across all 101 Swadesh concepts and 135 languages (ABTT-corrected k = 3), mean cross-lingual convergence is 0.58 ± 0.17, spanning a wide dynamic range from 0.12 (louse) to 0.91 (night). Grouping concepts into eight semantic categories exposes a robust concreteness and part-of-speech hierarchy mirroring Multi-SimLex (Vulić et al., 2020; Paper Figures 5 & 6): People (0.77 ± 0.05) and Nature (0.72 ± 0.15) converge most tightly, followed by Animals (0.60 ± 0.24), Properties (0.57 ± 0.11), Body parts (0.55 ± 0.17), Other (0.54 ± 0.20), and Actions/verbs (0.54 ± 0.13), while grammatical Pronouns/deictics rank lowest (0.45 ± 0.08).

A one-way ANOVA confirms that semantic category explains η² = 25.4% of total convergence variance (F(7, 93) = 4.53, p = 2.21 × 10−4; Kruskal-Wallis H = 28.00, p = 2.20 × 10−4)—more than twenty times the variance explained by surface orthography or phonology. This semantic hierarchy replicates on decontextualized native bare words (η² = 18.4%, F(7, 93) = 3.00, p = 0.007) and strengthens further in a partial F-test controlling simultaneously for subword fertility and word length (F(7, 91) = 7.74, p = 2.51 × 10−7).

Carrier-Frame vs. Decontextualized Bare-Word Baseline Paper §4.2 · Exp 2c · Fig. 8

Comparing concept convergence in the shared English carrier frame ("I saw a {word} near the river.") against decontextualized native bare words across all 101 concepts and 135 languages (Paper Figure 8, fig_carrier_baseline.pdf) demonstrates two complementary facts: first, the relative rank ordering of concepts is highly invariant across regimes (Spearman ρs = 0.867, p = 1.12 × 10−31); second, self-attention over shared English context tokens shifts mean absolute convergence upward by Δμ = +0.128 (from μbare = 0.46 to μctx = 0.58; paired t = 15.70, p = 9.87 × 10−29). Thus, the carrier frame acts as an additive baseline shift rather than generating the semantic hierarchy.

3.3 Ruling Out Surface-Form Confounds & Isolating Zipfian Subword Compactness Paper §4.3 · Exps 3a–3b · Figs. 9–10

Could high cross-lingual convergence merely reflect shared orthography, cognate roots, international loanwords, or subword tokenization length rather than conceptual alignment? Across all 92 Latin-script languages (4,186 language pairs), we regress ABTT-corrected convergence against orthographic similarity (1 − NLD), phonologically normalized similarity, character length, and SentencePiece subword fertility (Paper Figure 9, fig_swadesh_ranking.pdf). Neither orthographic overlap (R² = 0.012, p = 0.272), phonological similarity (R² = 0.000, p = 0.929), character length (R² = 0.000, p = 0.837), nor subword fertility (R² = 0.037, p = 0.055) explains Swadesh convergence: over 98.7% of convergence variance is independent of shared surface form.

Controlled POS-Matched & Strictly Tangible Concrete Nouns: Zipfian Subword Mediation

When we replace modern loanwords with 60 non-loanword concrete nouns (e.g., hammer, basket, axe, spider, needle; orthographic similarity 0.13) and compare them against Swadesh core concrete nouns (n = 52, orthographic similarity 0.12; Paper Figure 10a), Swadesh core nouns converge significantly more strongly both in raw embedding space (0.83 vs. 0.78; Mann-Whitney U = 2,016, p = 0.004, Cohen's d = 0.45) and after ABTT isotropy correction (0.75 vs. 0.65; U = 2,099, p = 8.40 × 10−4, Cohen's d = 0.54). Excluding 8 semi-abstract state nouns (anger, fear, hunger, thirst, dream, harvest, shadow, well) to form a strictly concrete 52-vs.-52 tangible noun comparison widens this separation even further (raw 0.83 vs. 0.76, U = 1,846, p = 7.16 × 10−4, d = 0.59; ABTT 0.75 vs. 0.63, U = 1,933, p = 8.03 × 10−5, d = 0.69).

Mechanistic Source (Zipf's Law of Abbreviation): Across world languages, culturally universal core nouns are predominantly monomorphemic roots and are significantly shorter than peripheral artifact nouns (4.19 vs. 4.85 characters, p = 9.02 × 10−9), resulting in fewer SentencePiece subword tokens per word (1.73 vs. 2.21 tokens/word). In an ANCOVA regressing ABTT convergence on the Swadesh indicator, subword fertility, and orthographic overlap, subword compactness strongly predicts convergence (t = −5.26, p = 7.43 × 10−7) and fully mediates the direct Swadesh indicator (p = 0.510)—showing how Zipfian lexical brevity translates into tighter atomic alignment in neural embedding space.

3.4 Genealogical Structure in Language-Pair Distances Paper §4.4 · Exps 4a–4b · Figs. 11–12

Having examined concept-level geometry after removing language offsets, we next test whether the language-pair distances Demb(ℓ, ℓ') themselves preserve historical phylogenetic relationships across all 135 languages (Paper Figure 11, fig_phylogenetic.pdf) and against external phonetic distances from the Automated Similarity Judgment Program (ASJP v20; Wichmann et al., 2022; Jäger, 2018) across the 88-language intersection (3,828 pairs; Paper Figure 12, fig_mantel_scatter.pdf).

Experiments 4a & 4b — Stratified Genealogical Correlation, LDND & RHM Asymmetry

Within language subfamilies (n = 321 pairs), NLLB-200's pairwise embedding distances track historical phonetic divergence with high fidelity under both standard ASJP NLD (ρs = 0.65, within-subfamily block-permutation p = 1.00 × 10−4, dyadic p = 2.40 × 10−40; bare-word ρs = 0.57, block p = 2.00 × 10−4) and Jäger's (2018) off-diagonal normalized LDND metric (ρs = 0.67; NLD–LDND agreement ρs = 0.983). Across n = 53 strictly cross-script within-subfamily pairs (e.g., Russian–Polish, Serbian–Croatian, Hindi–Urdu, Kazakh–Turkish), correlation remains equally strong (ρs = 0.57 in carrier frame, p = 6.95 × 10−6; ρs = 0.60 on bare words, p = 2.41 × 10−6; partial Mantel controlling for shared script ρpartial = 0.67). Because unrelated cross-family pairs (81.8% of all pairs) saturate near ceiling (ρs = 0.04), pooled global Mantel correlation is ρs = 0.13 (p = 0.020; LDND 0.15) in the carrier frame and nearly doubles to ρs = 0.24 (p = 0.001) on decontextualized native bare words.

3.5 Universal Colexification Structure (CLICS³ Across All 5,050 Pairs) Paper §4.5 · Exp 5 · Fig. 13

While Section 3.4 tests relationships between languages, our fifth study tests whether fine-grained distances between concepts reflect universal human conceptual associations documented in the CLICS³ database (List et al., 2018; Rzymski et al., 2020) across all C(101, 2) = 5,050 Swadesh concept pairs (Paper Figure 13, fig_colexification.pdf).

Concept pairs frequently colexified across ≥3 language families (n = 201, e.g., bark–skin, lie–sleep, man–person, drink–eat, earth–mountain, big–many) achieve a mean within-language cosine similarity of 0.37 vs. 0.29 for never-colexified pairs (n = 3,258; Mann-Whitney U = 492,477, p = 1.55 × 10−33, pooled Cohen's d = 1.43; continuous Spearman ρs = 0.13, p = 8.85 × 10−21).

  • Separating Accidental Homophony from Cross-Family Polysemy (List et al., 2018): Disaggregating across intermediate CLICS³ family tiers reveals a sharp step-function: pairs attested in only 1 language family (n = 1,241, μ = 0.30)—which frequently reflect accidental homophony or localized areal contact—show virtually zero excess similarity over never-colexified pairs (0 families, μ = 0.29), whereas similarity jumps sharply at the cross-family threshold of ≥3 families (μ = 0.37) and climbs monotonically across ≥5 families (n = 63, μ = 0.44) and ≥10 families (n = 31, μ = 0.48).
  • String-Distinct, Cognate-Excluded, Within-Category & Bare-Word Controls: Restricting exclusively to languages with strictly distinct word strings (wa,ℓ ≠ wb,ℓ) leaves the effect unchanged (0.37 vs. 0.29, U = 492,111, p = 2.14 × 10−33, d = 1.41), as does excluding phonetically similar cognate/derived forms with NLD < 0.2 (U = 491,254, p = 3.55 × 10−33, d = 1.40). Restricting to pairs within the same semantic category (n = 783) nearly triples the continuous rank correlation to ρs = 0.32 (p = 2.73 × 10−20, d = 1.26; within-POS d = 1.20), and evaluating decontextualized native bare words yields an even larger effect (d = 1.79, U = 528,369, p = 9.98 × 10−49; cross-lingual centroid similarity cos(μ̂a, μ̂b) d = 1.80 carrier, d = 1.71 bare).

Experiment 6a: Chromatic Subspace & the Berlin and Kay (1969) Evolutionary Hierarchy Paper Fig. 14

Projecting NLLB-200's cross-lingual centroids for all 11 basic color terms across 135 languages into PCA space (where PC1–PC3 capture 52.6% of variance: PC1 = 22.8%, PC2 = 16.3%, PC3 = 13.5%; Paper Figure 14, fig_color_circle.pdf) reveals two striking regularities:

Experiment 6b: Semantic Offset Invariance, Distance-Controlled Residuals & Doubly-Centered Coupling Paper §4.6 · Figs. 15–16

Finally, we test second-order relational geometry: do directional difference vectors dℓ(a,b) = eb,ℓ − ea,ℓ remain consistent across 135 languages (Paper Figures 15 & 16, fig_offset_combined.pdf and fig_offset_vector_demo.pdf)? Across 22 primary pairs (15 semantic antonym/gender/kinship pairs and 7 unrelated cross-domain negative controls), raw cosine consistency averages 0.84 (range 0.70 to 0.94, led by fire→water at 0.94).

Crucially, our additive model (Paper Equation 1) exposes a geometric baseline that must be controlled in any cross-lingual analogy test: because subtracting two embeddings from the same language automatically cancels the shared language vector ℓ̂, raw offset consistency correlates strongly with baseline centroid separation D = ||μ̂b − μ̂a||2 (r = 0.84, p = 1.03 × 10−6; analytical expectation E[S(a,b)] ≈ D / √(D² + σΔε²)), which mechanically inflates distant cross-domain negative controls (tree→red, stone→sleep). Controlling for centroid separation isolates genuine relational alignment across four complementary tests:

  • Linear, Leave-One-Out & Nonlinear Concave Residuals: Relative to the OLS trend on centroid distance, semantic pairs exhibit significantly positive residual consistency (μresid = +0.012, led by man→woman, big→small, fire→water, sun→moon, one→two) while negative controls fall systematically below trend (μresid = −0.025; Mann-Whitney U = 90, p = 0.003, Cohen's d = 1.06). Leave-one-language-out scoring yields identical separation (U = 89, p = 0.004), as does fitting the theoretical concave baseline D / √(D² + c) in both the carrier frame (U = 83, p = 0.016) and on bare words (U = 96, p = 6.00 × 10−4).
  • 100% Separation via Doubly-Centered Perturbation Coupling cos(ε̂a,ℓ, ε̂b,ℓ): Directly measuring how strongly language-specific residuals co-rotate after subtracting both ℓ̂ and μ̂c achieves complete 100% separation between the 15 semantic pairs and 7 controls in both the carrier frame (+0.17 vs. −0.00, U = 105/105, p = 5.86 × 10−6, led by man–woman +0.35, I–we +0.34, die–kill +0.24, eat–drink +0.21) and the bare-word regime (+0.23 vs. −0.01, U = 105/105, p = 5.86 × 10−6).
  • Distance- & POS-Matched Negative Controls & RHM Asymmetry: Against 15 never-colexified negative controls matched on both POS and centroid separation (||μ̂b − μ̂a||2 = 10.18 vs. 10.74, p = 0.30), semantic pairs achieve significantly higher raw consistency (0.82 vs. 0.78, U = 154, p = 0.045), residual consistency (U = 211, p = 3.55 × 10−5), and perturbation coupling (U = 225/225, p = 1.70 × 10−6). Across languages, high-resource languages align more tightly with cross-lingual centroid offsets than lower-resource languages (0.89 vs. 0.80, U = 3,101, p = 3.63 × 10−9).

4. Discussion, Neurocognitive Parallels & Conclusion Paper §5–§6

Taken together, these six experiments demonstrate that NLLB-200's shared encoder organizes multilingual lexical meaning through an approximately additive architecture, ec,ℓ = ē + μ̂c + ℓ̂ + ε̂c,ℓ, in which a low-dimensional language-identity shift ℓ̂ (PR = 13.3 on bare words) is superimposed onto a nearly three-times higher-dimensional conceptual manifold μ̂c (PR = 38.7; generalizing Chang et al., 2022). Subtracting per-language mean vectors increases concept separability by 2.14× on native bare words (1.15 → 2.46; 2.27× across strictly disjoint scripts) and by 1.19× in the shared carrier frame (2.25 → 2.69).

Neurocognitive & Psycholinguistic Parallels (Paper §5.1–§5.2)

  • Fronto-Temporal Core Language Network vs. ATL Conceptual Hub: NLLB-200's layer-wise hierarchy offers a functional parallel to the division of labor in the human brain between the left-lateralized fronto-temporal core language network (which processes lexical-syntactic structure across languages; Malik-Moraleda et al., 2022) and the bilateral amodal anterior temporal lobe (ATL) conceptual hub (Correia et al., 2014; Lambon Ralph et al., 2017), which is mirrored by the emergence of language-invariant conceptual centroids in Layers 6–10 before morphosyntactic features are unpacked for decoding at Layer 11.
  • Two-Level Bilingual Cortical Language Tuning (Chen et al., 2025): Our decomposition into a global language vector ℓ̂ plus a structured interaction residual ε̂c,ℓ (which exhibits 100% positive semantic pair co-rotation, +0.17 carrier / +0.23 bare vs. 0.00 controls) provides a direct mathematical analogue to recent bilingual fMRI evidence showing that cortical voxel tuning combines a global language-indicator shift with semantic-cluster-specific language tuning modulations (Chen et al., 2025).
  • Revised Hierarchical Model (RHM) Training-Resource Asymmetry (Kroll & Stewart, 1994): Just as L1 lexical representations maintain stronger, more direct links to the conceptual store than lower-proficiency L2 forms in the RHM, NLLB-200 embeds high-resource languages (n = 40) significantly closer to the shared cross-lingual conceptual manifold than lower-resource languages (n = 95) across Swadesh distances (0.24 vs. 0.30, p = 2.80 × 10−9), color centroids (1.42 vs. 2.05, p = 1.65 × 10−8), and relational offsets (0.89 vs. 0.80, p = 3.63 × 10−9).

Four Methodological Lessons for Probing Multilingual Models (Paper §5.2–§5.3)

  1. Pair Shared Carrier Frames with Decontextualized Bare Words: Shared context tokens reduce language-offset variance from 25.7% to 3.8% and shift absolute convergence upward by Δμ = +0.128 while attenuating global language-distance correlations (ρs = 0.13 vs. 0.24 on bare words).
  2. Control for Loanwords and Isolate Zipfian Subword Compactness: Modern international borrowings carry double the orthographic overlap of core vocabulary (0.26 vs. 0.13), while core Swadesh concrete nouns achieve higher convergence than non-loanword concrete nouns (d = 0.54–0.69) through Zipfian brevity and SentencePiece subword compactness (p = 7.43 × 10−7).
  3. Stratify Phylogenetic and Colexification Benchmarks by Horizon & Family Threshold: Global Mantel tests are attenuated by cross-family lexical saturation across 81.8% of pairs (ρs = 0.04 vs. within-subfamily NLD 0.65 / LDND 0.67), while CLICS³ requires ≥3 language families (0.37) to separate recurrent polysemy from single-family accidental homophony (0.30 vs. 0.29 baseline).
  4. Control for Centroid Separation in Relational Analogy Tests: Subtracting two vectors in the same language cancels ℓ̂, coupling raw cosine consistency to centroid distance ||μ̂b − μ̂a||2 (r = 0.84); distance-controlled residuals (d = 1.06) and doubly-centered perturbation coupling (p = 5.86 × 10−6) are essential to isolate true relational structure.
