## 1. General Assessment and Scientific Contribution

It is a rare pleasure to review a natural language processing manuscript that takes quantitative historical linguistics and computational lexical typology seriously—not merely as decorative motivation in the introduction, but as rigorous, falsifiable empirical ground truth for evaluating high-dimensional neural representations. Over the past decade, both of our research groups have worked to standardize cross-linguistic lexical and phonetic data through the Cross-Linguistic Data Formats (CLDF) initiative, the Concepticon reference catalog, the Database of Cross-Linguistic Colexifications ($\text{CLICS}^2$/$\text{CLICS}^3$; List et al., 2018; Rzymski et al., 2020), and global phylogenetic inference over the Automated Similarity Judgment Program (ASJP; Wichmann et al., 2022; Jäger, 2018). When NLP researchers import these resources, they frequently fall into classic comparative pitfalls: conflating surface loans with vertical cognacy, ignoring dyadic non-independence in distance matrices, mistaking accidental homophony in raw pivot glosses for genuine cross-linguistic polysemy, or comparing uncalibrated lexical sets across mismatched parts of speech.

This manuscript avoids almost every one of those traps. By probing the shared encoder of Meta’s 600M-parameter distilled NLLB-200 model across $N = 135$ typologically diverse languages and $M = 101$ Swadesh concepts—and systematically contrasting a contextualized English carrier-frame regime against a decontextualized native bare-word regime—Mathewson delivers a methodologically exemplary study of how statistical translation pressure shapes representation geometry.

From the perspective of comparative and historical linguistics, four findings stand out as major contributions:

1. **The Two-Way Additive Decomposition ($\mathbf{e}_{c,\ell} = \bar{\mathbf{e}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c + \hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$):** Demonstrating via exact sum-of-squares partitioning that native bare words encode a massive language-identity shift ($\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ accounting for $25.7\%$ of variance vs. $3.8\%$ in the shared carrier frame), and that subtracting $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ boosts the Conceptual Store Metric (CSM) by $2.14\times$ on bare words ($1.15 \to 2.46$, and $2.27\times$ across strictly disjoint scripts) to converge onto the same conceptual manifold as contextualized inputs ($2.25 \to 2.69$), resolves a long-standing tension in multilingual representation probing.
2. **Stratified Phylogenetic Signal and Deep-Time Saturation (Section 4.4):** The comparison against ASJP v20 phonetic distances across $88$ languages ($3{,}828$ pairs) is handled with admirable statistical maturity. Rather than reporting a naive global Mantel test and stopping there, the manuscript stratifies pairs by genealogical depth—showing why the strong within-subfamily signal ($\rho_s = 0.65$, $n = 321$ pairs) is attenuated globally ($\rho_s = 0.13$ contextualized; $\rho_s = 0.24$ on bare words) by the asymptotic ceiling saturation of lexical edit distance across unrelated language families ($81.8\%$ of all pairs, $\rho_s = 0.04$).
3. **Latent Cross-Linguistic Colexification on String-Distinct Pairs (Section 4.5):** The $\text{CLICS}^3$ evaluation across all $\binom{101}{2} = 5{,}050$ Swadesh concept pairs ($d = 1.43$, $p = 1.55 \times 10^{-33}$) is subjected to a brilliant control: restricting the within-language similarity $\bar{s}_{\text{within}}(a,b)$ strictly to languages where the two concepts are expressed by *distinct word forms* ($w_{a,\ell} \neq w_{b,\ell}$, $d = 1.41$; and $\text{NLD} \ge 0.2$, $d = 1.40$). This proves that NLLB-200 has internalized the continuous conceptual proximity underlying human colexification networks even in languages that lexicalize the two concepts separately.
4. **Zipfian Subword Compactness as the Mediator of Swadesh Stability (Section 4.3):** Showing that once modern international loanwords (\emph{coffee, radio, internet}; orthographic overlap $0.26$) and POS heterogeneity are controlled, the higher cross-lingual convergence of Swadesh core concrete nouns over non-loanword concrete nouns ($d = 0.54$--$0.69$) is fully mediated in ANCOVA by Zipfian word length ($4.19$ vs. $4.85$ characters) and SentencePiece subword fertility ($1.73$ vs. $2.21$ tokens/word; $t = -5.26, p = 7.43 \times 10^{-7}$) is a genuinely insightful bridge between quantitative typology and neural tokenization mechanics.

## 2. What We Especially Appreciate in the Current Methodological Controls

Before turning to constructive refinements, we want to highlight five specific controls in the current draft that directly address concerns historical linguists and lexical typologists would normally raise during review:

* **Within-Subfamily Block-Permutation & Cross-Script Phylogenetic Controls:** In comparative linguistics, standard Mantel permutations over a global matrix can be inflated by macro-clade block structure, while raw within-subfamily dyadic $p$-values ($p = 2.40 \times 10^{-40}$) violate row/column independence. Implementing a *within-subfamily block-permutation Mantel test* ($9{,}999$ permutations strictly within each subfamily, $p = 1.00 \times 10^{-4}$ in the carrier frame; $p = 2.00 \times 10^{-4}$ on bare words) alongside a *strictly cross-script within-subfamily test* ($n = 53$ pairs such as Russian–Polish, Serbian–Croatian, Hindi–Urdu, and Kazakh–Turkish; $\rho_s = 0.57$--$0.60$, partial Mantel $\rho_{\text{partial}} = 0.67$) completely rules out shared script and subword token identity as explanations for the phylogenetic signal.
* **The Deep-Time Saturation Explanation ($81.8\%$ Cross-Family Pairs):** As demonstrated in Jäger (2018), lexical phonetic distances lose phylogenetic resolution beyond the ~6,000–8,000 year time ceiling of the comparative method because cognate retention decays exponentially while chance phonological resemblances dominate. Figure 10 (`fig_mantel_scatter.pdf`) and Section 4.4 capture this decay curve across three regimes: within-subfamily ($\rho_s = 0.65$), cross-branch Indo-European ($\rho_s = 0.10$, $n = 374$), and between-family saturation ($\rho_s = 0.04$, $n = 3{,}133$).
* **Within-Category ($\rho_s = 0.32$) and Bare-Word ($d = 1.79$) $\text{CLICS}^3$ Controls:** Because colexifications naturally occur more frequently between concepts in the same semantic field or part of speech, an unstratified correlation ($\rho_s = 0.13$) actually *understates* the local gradient because cross-category colexifications (e.g., *ear–hear*, *bark–skin*) sit at larger baseline embedding distances than non-colexified within-category pairs. Showing that restricting to within-category pairs ($n = 783$) nearly triples the continuous Spearman correlation to $\rho_s = 0.32$ ($p = 2.73 \times 10^{-20}$) and that decontextualized bare words yield $d = 1.79$ ($p = 9.98 \times 10^{-49}$) is thoroughly convincing.
* **Berlin & Kay (1969) Wildcard Sensitivity & Length Partial Correlations:** Treating *grey* accurately as a Berlin & Kay wildcard (testing canonical 10-term $\rho_s = 0.93$, midpoint stage $5.0$ $r = 0.89$, and Stage VII $r = 0.82$), replicating on bare words ($\rho_s = 0.91$), and partials controlling for cross-lingual word length ($r_{\text{partial}} = 0.70, p = 0.017$) preempts the standard critique that evolutionary color stages merely proxy word frequency or length.
* **Analytical Baseline Correction for Relational Offsets:** Proving mathematically and empirically that subtracting two vectors in language $\ell$ ($\mathbf{d}_\ell(a,b) = \mathbf{e}_{b,\ell} - \mathbf{e}_{a,\ell}$) cancels the shared language vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ and mechanically couples raw cosine consistency $S(a,b)$ to centroid separation $D = \|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_b - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_a\|_2$ ($r = 0.84$) is a major methodological contribution to NLP analogy evaluation. The doubly-centered perturbation coupling test $\cos(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{a,\ell}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{b,\ell})$ ($+0.17$ vs. $-0.00$, $U = 105/105, p = 5.86 \times 10^{-6}$) is an elegant solution.

## 3. High-Leverage Refinements (Theoretical Precision & Fast Empirical Checks)

While the manuscript is already in outstanding shape, incorporating four targeted refinements—each requiring only a few sentences of theoretical precision or a trivial check on the existing data—would make historical linguists and computational typologists even more enthusiastic about this work.

### 3.1. NLD vs. LDND in ASJP (Jäger 2018; Wichmann et al. 2010) and Vertical Inheritance vs. Contact
In Section 3.2 (line 57) and `backend/app/benchmarks.py` (line 1267), ASJP lexical distance is computed as the mean Normalized Levenshtein Distance ($\text{NLD}(a,b) = \text{lev}(a,b)/\max(|a|,|b|)$) across shared 40-item Swadesh concepts. In the ASJP literature (Wichmann et al., 2010; Jäger, 2018), raw mean NLD is typically normalized a second time by dividing by the mean NLD of *off-diagonal* (non-synonymous) concept pairs between the two doculects—yielding **LDND** (*Levenshtein Distance Normalized Divided*)—in order to factor out accidental phonological similarity between unrelated languages that happen to share small phoneme inventories or similar syllable canons (e.g., Polynesian vs. Japanese).
* **Fast Empirical Verification on Your Dataset:** Computing exact LDND alongside NLD across your $88$ ASJP languages ($3{,}828$ pairs) reveals that NLD and LDND are nearly identical within subfamilies ($\rho_s = 0.983$ across the $n = 321$ within-subfamily pairs), and replacing NLD with LDND actually *improves* your within-subfamily contextualized correlation from $\rho_s = 0.65$ to $\rho_s = 0.67$ (bare-word $\rho_s = 0.57$) and improves your global contextualized Mantel correlation from $\rho_s = 0.13$ to $\rho_s = 0.15$! Mentioning in a brief parenthetical in Section 3.2/4.4 that results are invariant (and slightly stronger: $\rho_s = 0.67$ within-subfamily, $\rho_s = 0.15$ global) under Jäger’s (2018) off-diagonal LDND normalization will immediately satisfy any ASJP specialist.
* **Vertical Inheritance (Cognacy) vs. Horizontal Contact (Areal Borrowing):** In Section 4.4 or 5.2, we recommend adding two sentences clarifying that pairwise language distances in NLLB-200 ($D_{\text{emb}}(\ell, \ell')$) reflect a composite of **genealogical inheritance (shared cognates and morphosyntactic typology)** and **horizontal areal contact (*Sprachbund* convergence and lexical borrowing)**—for instance, Perso-Arabic lexical strata shared across Indo-Iranian and Turkic, or Sanskritic loans across South and Southeast Asia. Why does the within-subfamily correlation with ASJP remain so strong ($\rho_s = 0.65$) despite areal contact? Because your probe is restricted to the 101-item Swadesh basic vocabulary—which Swadesh (1952) and Tadmor et al. (2010, Loanword Typology Project) specifically showed is maximally resistant to horizontal borrowing—and because ASJP distances are evaluated on the 40-item ultra-stable subset. Explicitly noting this synergy between Swadesh’s borrowability resistance and ASJP’s genealogical signal tightens the theoretical narrative.

### 3.2. Accidental Homophony vs. Conceptual Polysemy in $\text{CLICS}^3$ (Why the $\ge 3$ Family Threshold Matters)
In Section 4.2 (line 111), you rightly point out that English stimulus homonymy/polysemy depresses cross-lingual convergence for English pivot strings like *bark* (tree cortex vs. dog vocalization), *lie* (recline vs. falsehood), and *fly* (insect vs. verb of motion). Then, in Section 4.5, you use $\text{CLICS}^3$ (List et al., 2018; Rzymski et al., 2020) with a threshold of $\ge 3$ language families ($n = 201$ pairs) to benchmark conceptual similarity.
* **Connecting the Two Phenomena:** In $\text{CLICS}^2$ and $\text{CLICS}^3$, we introduced the threshold of $\ge 3$ distinct language families (and genealogical family weighting) specifically to filter out **accidental homophony** (such as English *bark* or *lie*, which are historical phonetic mergers restricted to a single language or branch) as well as dataset-specific transcription artifacts, isolating recurrent **conceptual polysemy / colexification** driven by universal human cognitive associations (François, 2008; List et al., 2018).
* **Fast Empirical Verification in `docs/data/colexification.json`:** If you inspect the intermediate frequency tiers in your own `colexification.json` across all $5{,}050$ pairs, a remarkable pattern emerges:
  - $0$ families (never colexified, $n = 3{,}258$): mean cosine similarity $\mu = 0.289$
  - $1$ family (single-family attestation / homophony noise, $n = 1{,}241$): $\mu = 0.295$ (virtually identical to $0$ families!)
  - $2$ families ($n = 350$): $\mu = 0.311$ (pooled $1$–$2$ families, $n = 1{,}591$: $\mu = 0.299$)
  - $\ge 3$ families (the standard $\text{CLICS}^3$ threshold, $n = 201$): $\mu = 0.369$
  - $\ge 5$ families ($n = 63$): $\mu = 0.437$
  - $\ge 10$ families ($n = 31$): $\mu = 0.476$
  Reporting this sharp nonlinear step from $1$ family ($\mu = 0.295$) to $\ge 3$ families ($\mu = 0.369$) and $\ge 10$ families ($\mu = 0.476$) in Section 4.5 provides an extraordinary mutual validation of both NLLB-200 and the $\text{CLICS}^3$ methodology: single-family colexifications (which mix accidental homophony and areal idiosyncrasies) show almost no excess embedding similarity over never-colexified pairs ($+0.006$), whereas recurrent cross-family colexifications ($\ge 3$ families) exhibit a massive conceptual proximity jump!
* **Highlighting "Latent Conceptual Colexification":** In Section 5.2, emphasize the theoretical significance of your string-distinct control ($w_{a,\ell} \neq w_{b,\ell}$, $d = 1.41$ contextualized, $d = 1.79$ bare words). In lexical typology, a language $\ell$ either colexifies $(a,b)$ or dislexifies them into two words. Your string-distinct test proves that even when language $\ell$ *dislexifies* $a$ and $b$ into two phonologically unrelated lexemes, NLLB-200’s multilingual training objective forces the representations $\mathbf{e}_{a,\ell}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{b,\ell}$ to sit closer along a continuous conceptual manifold because *other* languages in the training pool colexify or contextually substitute them.

### 3.3. Swadesh’s Stability Hierarchy, Zipf’s Law of Abbreviation, and Morphological Simplexity
In Section 4.3 (lines 166–169), you show that Swadesh core concrete nouns are significantly shorter ($4.19$ vs. $4.85$ characters, $p = 9.02 \times 10^{-9}$) and have lower SentencePiece subword fertility ($1.73$ vs. $2.21$ tokens/word) than non-loanword peripheral concrete nouns (*hammer, basket, needle*), and that subword fertility mediates the Swadesh convergence advantage in ANCOVA ($t = -5.26, p = 7.43 \times 10^{-7}$).
* **Deepening the Typological Interpretation:** From a diachronic and morphological perspective, why are Swadesh core nouns shorter and segmented into fewer subwords across 135 languages? Not only because of Zipf’s (1935, 1949) Law of Abbreviation (usage frequency driving phonological reduction), but because Swadesh (1952) core concepts are overwhelmingly **morphologically simplex, unanalyzable monomorphemic roots**, whereas peripheral concrete artifacts and biological taxa (*hammer, basket, spider, needle, fireplace*) are cross-linguistically much more likely to be lexicalized via **polymorphemic compounds, derivational affixes, or descriptive circumlocutions** (i.e., partial colexifications / *motifs* in the sense of François, 2008 and List, 2023). When SentencePiece segments a polymorphemic or derived peripheral noun into multiple subwords ($2.21$ tokens/word) and those subwords are mean-pooled, language-specific derivational morphology and modifier roots inject compositional variance into the embedding. By contrast, monomorphemic Swadesh roots ($1.73$ tokens/word) map more often to atomic lexical representations. Adding a sentence connecting Zipfian brevity to *morphological simplexity (monomorphemic roots vs. derived/compound peripheral nouns)* enriches Section 4.3 and Section 5.2.

## 5. Summary Verdict

This is a landmark study at the interface of mechanistic interpretability, cognitive science, and quantitative linguistic typology. The statistical rigor, the self-critical confound controls (from loanword and POS matching to cross-script subfamily Mantel tests, string-distinct colexification tests, and doubly-centered perturbation coupling), and the open-source `InterpretCognates` suite set a new standard for evaluating multilingual language models against linguistic universals. We strongly recommend acceptance.
