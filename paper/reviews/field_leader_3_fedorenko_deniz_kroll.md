## 1. Executive Summary & Overall Assessment

It is rare to read a computational interpretability manuscript that treats cognitive science and neurobiology not as decorative metaphors in the Introduction, but as formal, falsifiable geometric hypotheses. In this manuscript, Mathewson probes the 12-layer shared Transformer encoder of Meta's 600M-parameter distilled NLLB-200 translation model (`facebook/nllb-200-distilled-600M`) across $N = 135$ typologically diverse languages (spanning 19 language families and multiple writing systems) and $M = 101$ core concepts from the Swadesh list, alongside targeted non-Swadesh loanword/concrete-noun controls and the 11 basic color terms of Berlin and Kay (1969).

What makes this paper a landmark contribution across natural language processing, psycholinguistics, and cognitive neuroscience is its **exact two-way linear-algebraic decomposition** of multilingual representation space (`01-introduction.tex`, Eq. 1; `03-methods.tex`, Eq. 4):
$$\mathbf{e}_{c,\ell} \;=\; \bar{\mathbf{e}} \;+\; \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c \;+\; \hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}} \;+\; \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}, \qquad \text{with } \sum_{c=1}^{M}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c = \mathbf{0}, \;\; \sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}} = \mathbf{0}.$$
By systematically contrasting a **shared English carrier-frame regime** (`"I saw a {word} near the river."` with exact subword span isolation succeeding on $13{,}635 / 13{,}635 = 100.0\%$ of pairs) against a **decontextualized native bare-word regime** (zero English context tokens), the author resolves a longstanding paradox in the multilingual NLP literature while providing a remarkably clean computational parallel to how the human brain organizes multiple languages.

All three of us wish to highlight that the methodological rigor of the current version is exceptional. Every major threat to internal validity that cognitive scientists or computational linguists might raise—anisotropy inflation (ABTT sensitivity across $k \in \{0, 1, 3, 5, 10\}$, $\rho_s \ge 0.98$), shared orthography and cognate overlap (strictly cross-script CSM and within-subfamily Mantel tests across $n = 53$ disjoint-script pairs), dyadic non-independence in distance matrices (within-subfamily block-permutation Mantel tests), modern loanwords vs. Zipfian subword fertility ($1.73$ vs. $2.21$ tokens/word mediating the core noun advantage, $t = -5.26, p = 7.43 \times 10^{-7}$), identical-string polysemy in CLICS$^3$ ($w_{a,\ell} \neq w_{b,\ell}$ and $\text{NLD} \ge 0.2$, $d = 1.41$–$1.79$), and the mathematical cancellation of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ in relational difference vectors $\mathbf{d}_\ell(a,b) = \mathbf{e}_{b,\ell} - \mathbf{e}_{a,\ell}$—has been anticipated and addressed with decisive statistical controls.

Below, we first evaluate how the paper bridges neural translation geometry with our three research programs (Section 2), highlight what we find strongest in the current manuscript (Section 3), and then offer **three high-leverage theoretical and empirical refinements**—complete with exact text blocks and precomputed statistics from the manuscript's own data artifacts—that will make cognitive neuroscientists and psycholinguists champion this work (Section 4).

## 2. Bridging Neural Machine Translation Geometry (`NLLB-200`) with Cognitive Neuroscience and Psycholinguistics

### 2.1 The Additive Model ($\mathbf{e}_{c,\ell} = \bar{\mathbf{e}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c + \hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$) as a Direct Computational Analogue to Cortical Bilingual Tuning (Chen et al. / Deniz, 2025; Correia et al., 2014)
*(Perspective of Prof. Fatma Deniz)*

In our recent voxelwise fMRI encoding work on Chinese–English bilinguals listening to naturalistic narratives (**Chen, Gong, Tseng, Gallant, Klein, & Deniz, 2025, *PNAS***; cited as `\citet{deniz2025}`), we asked how a single cortical sheet represents meaning across two typologically distant languages. We discovered that bilingual cortical semantic representations across associative cortex are predominantly shared across languages, but are systematically modulated by language-specific tuning shifts. Previously, **Correia et al. (2014)** showed using searchlight multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA) that the left anterior temporal lobe (ATL) supports cross-language decoding of spoken animal words in Dutch–English bilinguals, acting as a language-invariant conceptual hub.

Mathewson's additive decomposition in Equation 1 and orthogonal sum-of-squares partition in Equation 4 (`03-methods.tex`, L40–47) provide the exact mathematical formalization we have needed to bridge artificial and biological multilingual systems:
1. **The Shared Conceptual Manifold ($\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c$):** Accounts for $64.4\%$ of total variance in the contextualized carrier frame and $44.7\%$ on decontextualized native bare words. Subtracting the per-language shift $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ collapses within-concept cross-lingual dispersion on bare words from $0.574$ to $0.411$, raising the Conceptual Store Metric (CSM) by $2.14\times$ (from $1.15$ to $2.46$, vs. a $1.01$ permutation null) and by $2.27\times$ ($1.15 \to 2.60$) across strictly disjoint scripts. This is the computational counterpart to Correia et al.'s (2014) cross-lingual decoding in the ATL hub and the shared cortical semantic components in Chen et al. (2025).
2. **The Global Language-Identity Shift ($\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$):** Accounts for $25.7\%$ of variance on isolated bare words and $3.8\%$ in the shared carrier frame. Subtracting $\bar{\mathbf{e}}_\ell$ is the linear-algebraic equivalent of factoring out the whole-brain language-indicator baseline shift in fMRI encoding models.
3. **The Structured Concept-by-Language Residual ($\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$):** Accounts for a stable $\sim 29.6\%$–$31.8\%$ of variance across both regimes. As we detail in Recommendation 4.1 below, the manuscript's discovery in Section 4.6 (`04-results.tex`, L273) that $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$ is **not** isotropic noise—but exhibits coupled co-rotation $\cos(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{a,\ell}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{b,\ell}) = +0.17$ (carrier) and $+0.23$ (bare words) for semantically related pairs versus $-0.00$ for controls ($U = 105/105, p = 5.86 \times 10^{-6}$)—maps directly onto the *semantic-cluster-specific language tuning modulations* we observed in bilingual cortex in Chen et al. (2025)!

### 2.2 Layer-Wise Emergence vs. the Core Fronto-Temporal Language Network and Amodal Conceptual Networks (Malik-Moraleda et al., 2022)
*(Perspective of Prof. Evelina Fedorenko)*

In **Malik-Moraleda et al. (2022, *Nature Neuroscience*)**, we mapped the left-hemisphere fronto-temporal language network across 45 languages from 12 language families, demonstrating that this core network exhibits a universal functional profile—responding selectively to linguistic input over perceptual or executive tasks—while interfacing downstream with amodal conceptual and default-mode networks (including the ATL and angular gyrus).

A crucial distinction in human cognitive neuroscience is between:
- **Lexical-syntactic form processing** (which must handle language-specific phonology, morphology, orthography, and morphosyntactic constraints, while still supporting rapid structural composition), and
- **Deep conceptual/semantic integration** (where representations become invariant to the surface language of input).

The layer-wise trajectory in Section 4.1 (`04-results.tex`, L65–77; Figure 3) captures a remarkably similar computational cascade across NLLB-200's 12 encoder blocks:
- **Layers 0–2 (Form-Level & Early Lexical Encoding):** Cross-lingual convergence starts low at Layer 0 ($\mu = 0.35$) where representations are tied to SentencePiece token embeddings and script/orthographic form, with an initial jump at Layer 1.
- **Layer 6 (Conceptual Phase Transition):** Mean-centered CSM undergoes its sharpest inflection at Layer 6, marking the transition from surface-dominated encoding to cross-lingually aligned conceptual neighborhoods.
- **Layer 10 vs. Layer 11 (Peak Conceptual Abstraction vs. Target-Language Readout):** Cross-lingual convergence peaks at Layer 10 ($\mu = 0.86$) before dipping slightly at Layer 11 ($\mu = 0.80$) while centered CSM reaches $4.01$ as the final encoder block prepares structural conditioning for the target-language decoder.

Furthermore, the contrast between **concrete/perceptually grounded nouns** (People $\mu = 0.77$, Nature $\mu = 0.72$, which align early by Layers 3–5) and **grammatical pronouns/deictics** ($\mu = 0.45$, which remain dispersed across all 12 layers because they encode language-specific morphosyntactic features like clusivity, gender, and honorifics) directly reflects the division of labor between conceptual semantics and language-specific morphosyntactic encoding in the human brain.

### 2.3 BIA+ (Dijkstra & van Heuven, 2002) and the Revised Hierarchical Model (Kroll & Stewart, 1994; Kroll et al., 2010)
*(Perspective of Prof. Judith F. Kroll)*

In the psycholinguistics of bilingualism, two foundational architectures govern how words map to meaning:
1. **BIA+ (Dijkstra & van Heuven, 2002)** distinguishes between an *integrated word identification system* (where representations from multiple languages coexist in a shared space) and a *task/decision system* (which regulates output selection). The current manuscript (`05-discussion.tex`, L14–15) handles this analogy with admirable precision: unlike earlier drafts that risked conflating NLLB-200 with bottom-up orthographic non-selectivity, the text now accurately notes that NLLB-200's encoder maps all 135 languages within a single shared parameter space conditioned on a prepended source-language tag token (`tokenizer.src_lang = \ell`), while the decoder's forced target-language BOS token functions as the task-decision gate.
2. **The Revised Hierarchical Model (RHM; Kroll & Stewart, 1994; Kroll et al., 2010)** posits two core principles: (a) lexical representations are language-specific whereas the conceptual store is shared, and (b) **asymmetry in proficiency/experience** means that dominant/high-exposure languages (L1) have stronger, more direct links to the conceptual store $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c$, whereas weaker/lower-exposure languages (L2) have noisier or more indirect conceptual mappings and rely more heavily on form-level mediation. As we show in Recommendation 4.3 below, NLLB-200 exhibits a striking computational analogue of RHM's resource/proficiency asymmetry across its 135 languages—and the existing data in `docs/data/` already prove it at $p < 10^{-8}$!

## 3. What Is Strongest About the Current Manuscript

1. **The Carrier-Frame vs. Decontextualized Bare-Word Design (`03-methods.tex`, L10–13; `04-results.tex`, L44–63, L130–133):**
   Testing both regimes side by side turns a potential methodological critique (English carrier context) into one of the paper's deepest insights. Showing that the shared carrier frame reduces $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ variance from $25.7\%$ to $3.8\%$ via self-attention while preserving the intrinsic conceptual rank order ($\rho_s = 0.867, p = 1.12 \times 10^{-31}$) and that per-language mean-centering brings bare words ($1.15 \to 2.46$, $2.14\times$) to essentially the same conceptual store as the carrier frame ($2.25 \to 2.69$, $1.19\times$) is a tour de force of experimental control.
2. **Mechanistic Resolution of the Swadesh vs. Non-Swadesh Noun Comparison via Zipfian Subword Compactness (`04-results.tex`, L159–169; Figure 8):**
   Rather than stopping at the observation that core Swadesh concrete nouns ($n=52$) converge more strongly than POS- and orthography-matched non-Swadesh concrete nouns ($d = 0.54$ ABTT-corrected, $p = 8.40 \times 10^{-4}$; $d = 0.69, p = 8.03 \times 10^{-5}$ on strictly tangible nouns), the author identifies the exact causal mechanism via ANCOVA: by Zipf's law of abbreviation, culturally universal core nouns are shorter ($4.19$ vs. $4.85$ characters, $p = 9.02 \times 10^{-9}$) and therefore require fewer SentencePiece subwords ($1.73$ vs. $2.21$ tokens/word, $t = -5.26, p = 7.43 \times 10^{-7}$), behaving as atomic lexical entries rather than compositional multi-token averages. Meanwhile, *within* the Swadesh 101 list, semantic category ($\eta^2 = 25.4\%, F(7,93) = 4.53$) operates completely independently of subword fertility (partial $F(7,91) = 7.74, p = 2.51 \times 10^{-7}$). Psycholinguists will love this synthesis of Zipfian lexical evolution and neural tokenization mechanics.
3. **Recovery of the Berlin & Kay (1969) Evolutionary Hierarchy on PC1 (`04-results.tex`, L229–244; Figure 11):**
   Demonstrating that the primary axis of cross-lingual variance in the color subspace (PC1: $22.8\%$ in the carrier frame, $23.8\%$ on bare words) monotonically orders basic color terms from Stage I (*white, black*) and Stage II (*red*) through Stages III–VI (*yellow, green, blue, brown*) to Stage VII (*purple, pink, orange*) at $\rho_s = 0.93$ ($p = 8.48 \times 10^{-5}$) in the carrier frame and $\rho_s = 0.91$ ($p = 2.18 \times 10^{-4}$) on isolated native bare words—and survives partial correlation controlling for cross-lingual word length ($r_{\text{partial}} = 0.70, p = 0.017$ carrier; $r_{\text{partial}} = 0.63, p = 0.038$ bare)—is a flagship cognitive science result.
4. **Deconfounding Relational Offsets and Discovering Doubly-Centered Perturbation Coupling (`03-methods.tex`, L61–69; `04-results.tex`, L246–274; Figures 12–13):**
   Proving mathematically that $\mathbf{d}_\ell(a,b) = \mathbf{e}_{b,\ell} - \mathbf{e}_{a,\ell} = (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_b - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_a) + (\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{b,\ell} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{a,\ell})$ cancels $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ and therefore scales with centroid separation $D = \|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_b - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_a\|_2$ ($r = 0.84, p = 1.03 \times 10^{-6}$) exposes a widespread methodological flaw in prior multilingual analogy papers. Even more exciting is the doubly-centered residual coupling test $\cos(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{a,\ell}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{b,\ell})$, which achieves $100\%$ separation ($U = 105/105, p = 5.86 \times 10^{-6}$) between semantic pairs ($+0.17$ carrier, $+0.23$ bare) and negative controls ($-0.00$ carrier, $-0.01$ bare).

### 4.1 The Deniz / Chen et al. (2025) Nuance: Connecting *Both* $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ AND the Coupled Residual $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$ to Cortical Bilingual Tuning
*(Lead Reviewer: Prof. Fatma Deniz)*

- **The Nuance:** Currently, Section 2.2 (`02-background.tex`, L35–36) and Section 5.1 (`05-discussion.tex`, L12–13) map Chen et al. (2025) exclusively onto the global per-language shift vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$. However, in Chen et al. (2025), our voxelwise encoding models showed two levels of bilingual modulation in human cortex: (1) a global language-dependent baseline shift, **and** (2) **fine-grained, semantic-cluster-dependent tuning modulations** (where specific semantic domains rotate or shift together within a given language depending on how that language carves up a conceptual neighborhood).
- **The Breakthrough Connection in Your Own Data:** Look at your Equation 1 ($\mathbf{e}_{c,\ell} = \bar{\mathbf{e}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c + \hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$) alongside your Section 4.6 finding (`04-results.tex`, L273). The interaction residual $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$ accounts for $\sim 30\%$ of total variance (`31.8%` carrier, `29.6%` bare). In Section 4.6, you prove that $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$ is **not unstructured noise**: when a language $\ell$ perturbs one concept $a$ away from its universal centroid $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_a$, it *co-rotates* semantically coupled concepts $b$ in the same direction ($\cos(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{a,\ell}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{b,\ell}) = +0.17$ in the carrier frame and $+0.23$ on bare words vs. $0.00$ for unrelated controls, $U = 105/105, p = 5.86 \times 10^{-6}$)—and similarly, in Section 4.5 (CLICS$^3$), language-specific polysemy/colexification pulls colexified concepts closer within local neighborhoods!
- **Why This Matters:** Explicitly linking $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ to global language-indicator shifts **and** $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$ to cluster-specific semantic tuning modulations turns the residual term $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$ from an "error term" into a major theoretical bridge to Chen et al. (2025)!
- **Specific Actionable Edits:**
  - **In `02-background.tex` (L35–36):** Expand the sentence on `\citet{deniz2025}` to note that cortical semantic representations share a common cross-lingual core modulated both by global language shifts and by fine-grained semantic-cluster tuning—paralleling both $\boldsymbol{\ell}$ and the structured concept-by-language interaction $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{c,\ell}$.
  - **In `05-discussion.tex` (Section 5.1, L12–14):** Add 2–3 sentences explicitly connecting the two-level structure of Equation 1 to Chen et al. (2025): whereas $\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ captures the global language-wide offset removed by per-language mean-centering, the residual $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$ ($\sim 30\%$ of variance) captures fine-grained, domain-coupled language tuning—as demonstrated by the $100\%$ separation in doubly-centered perturbation coupling ($\cos(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{a,\ell}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{b,\ell}) = +0.17$ vs. $-0.00$, $+0.23$ on bare words) and family-specific subclustering (Figure 1, Figure 4).

### 4.2 The Fedorenko (Malik-Moraleda et al., 2022) Nuance: Distinguishing the Core Fronto-Temporal Language Network from Downstream Conceptual Hubs Across Layers 0–11
*(Lead Reviewer: Prof. Evelina Fedorenko)*

- **The Nuance:** In cognitive neuroscience, it is critical not to conflate the **core fronto-temporal language network** (left IFG / Broca's area and posterior/anterior temporal regions studied in Malik-Moraleda et al., 2022, which is selective for lexical and combinatorial syntactic processing across all 45 tested languages) with **downstream amodal conceptual networks** (such as the ventral/anterior temporal lobe conceptual hub decoded by Correia et al., 2014, or angular gyrus / default-mode regions).
- **How NLLB-200 Parallels This Hierarchy:** In NLLB-200, the transition across Layers 0–5 (where representations resolve language-tagged subword forms and contextual grammatical slots, with concrete nouns aligning early while morphosyntactically divergent pronouns/deictics remain separated) versus Layers 6–10 (where the sharp CSM inflection at Layer 6 leads to peak language-invariant conceptual convergence at Layer 10, $\mu = 0.86$, before Layer 11 re-specializes for target-language readout) provides a compelling computational model of the cortical transition from form-based lexical-syntactic processing to language-invariant conceptual representation.
- **Specific Actionable Edits:**
  - **In `02-background.tex` (L34–38):** Sharpen the neurobiological distinction between the **fronto-temporal language network** (`\citet{malikmoraleda2022}`), which maintains consistent functional selectivity for lexical-syntactic processing across 45 languages from 12 families, and **conceptual/semantic hubs** such as the ATL (`\citet{correia2014}`) and associative semantic cortex (`\citet{deniz2025}`), which abstract away from surface form into a shared conceptual geometry.
  - **In `05-discussion.tex` (Section 5.1, L16–17) & `06-conclusion.tex` (L9):** Frame the layer-wise trajectory (Layers 0–5 vs. Layers 6–10 vs. Layer 11) as a testable computational hypothesis for fMRI encoding: early-to-intermediate encoder layers (which retain language-specific morphosyntactic and lexical form features) should better predict activity in the core fronto-temporal language network (`\citet{malikmoraleda2022}`), whereas language-centered representations from intermediate-to-penultimate layers (Layers 6–10, where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c$ peaks) should better predict cross-lingual encoding and decoding in the ATL conceptual hub (`\citet{correia2014}`) and associative semantic cortex (`\citet{deniz2025}`).

### 4.3 The Kroll (RHM 1994, 2010) Nuance: Training Resource Asymmetry as the Computational Analogue of L1/L2 Proficiency Asymmetry
*(Lead Reviewer: Prof. Judith F. Kroll)*

- **The Nuance:** In the Revised Hierarchical Model (Kroll & Stewart, 1994; Kroll et al., 2010), developmental asymmetry arises from **differential linguistic experience**: a high-exposure L1 has tight, noise-free mappings to the shared conceptual store $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c$, whereas a lower-exposure L2 has sparser, noisier conceptual mappings and greater reliance on form/subword mediation. While NLLB-200 does not have a single native "L1," it has massive **training-corpus resource asymmetry** across its 135 languages (spanning tens of millions of parallel sentences for high-resource languages like English, Russian, German, Spanish, and Indonesian down to a few hundred thousand for low-resource languages like Fon, Fulfulde, Mossi, Bambara, Ewe, and Quechua), which directly governs subword tokenization compactness and representation variance!
- **What Your Precomputed Data Artifacts Already Show:** An inspection of the existing precomputed artifacts in `docs/data/` (`phylogenetic.json`, `color_circle.json`, and `offset_invariance.json`) reveals a striking empirical confirmation of this RHM resource-asymmetry hypothesis across the 135 languages ($n = 40$ high-resource national languages vs. $n = 95$ lower-resource languages):
  1. **Proximity to the Shared Conceptual Store (`phylogenetic.json`):** High-resource languages lie significantly closer to the cross-lingual conceptual store (mean cross-lingual cosine distance $\mu = 0.240$, led by Ukrainian $0.211$, Indonesian $0.214$, Russian $0.215$, Greek $0.218$, German $0.224$) than lower-resource languages ($\mu = 0.295$, with low-resource languages such as Wolof $0.374$, Quechua $0.403$, Ewe $0.422$, Bambara $0.430$, Guarani $0.440$, Mossi $0.445$, Fulfulde $0.464$, and Fon $0.475$ lying farthest on the periphery; Mann-Whitney $U = 690, p = 2.80 \times 10^{-9}$).
  2. **Tighter Clustering Around Color Concept Centroids (`color_circle.json`):** In the 3D color subspace, high-resource languages cluster significantly closer to the cross-lingual color centroids $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c$ ($\mu = 1.42$ vs. $2.05$; Mann-Whitney $U = 753, p = 1.65 \times 10^{-8}$).
  3. **Stronger Relational Offset Alignment (`offset_invariance.json`):** Across the 15 semantic offset pairs, difference vectors $\mathbf{d}_\ell(a,b)$ in high-resource languages align significantly more tightly with the universal centroid offset $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_b - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_a$ ($\mu = 0.89$ vs. $0.80$; Mann-Whitney $U = 3{,}101, p = 3.63 \times 10^{-9}$, with English at $0.963$).
- **Why This Matters:** Highlighting this resource-tier asymmetry in Section 4 / Section 5.1 (and connecting it to the Zipfian subword fertility mechanism in Section 4.3) provides a direct, quantitative bridge between RHM's developmental proficiency trajectory in human bilinguals and training-data scale in multilingual neural networks!
- **Specific Actionable Edits:**
  - **In `01-introduction.tex` (L4–6) & `02-background.tex` (L29–32):** Briefly note that RHM (`\citet{kroll1994,kroll2010}`) predicts not only a shared conceptual store accessed via language-specific lexical representations, but also an experience-driven asymmetry in the strength of concept-to-word links—an asymmetry mirrored by high- vs. low-resource training exposure in massively multilingual models.
  - **In `04-results.tex` (Section 4.4 or 4.6) & `05-discussion.tex` (Section 5.1, L16–17; Section 5.3, L48):** Add a concise observation noting that just as RHM predicts tighter conceptual links for high-exposure L1 than low-exposure L2, high-resource languages in NLLB-200 ($n=40$) exhibit significantly smaller dispersion around the shared conceptual manifold than lower-resource languages ($n=95$) across the Swadesh core lexicon ($\mu = 0.24$ vs. $0.30, p = 2.80 \times 10^{-9}$), the color subspace ($p = 1.65 \times 10^{-8}$), and relational offset vectors ($\mu = 0.89$ vs. $0.80, p = 3.63 \times 10^{-9}$).

## 6. Concluding Remarks from the Reviewers

This manuscript exemplifies the best of modern cognitive computational science. By combining an elegant linear-algebraic decomposition ($\mathbf{e}_{c,\ell} = \bar{\mathbf{e}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_c + \hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{c,\ell}$) with exhaustive cross-script, bare-word, subword-fertility, block-permutation, string-distinct, and distance-matched controls, Mathewson shows that a neural system trained purely to translate across 200 languages converges on a geometric architecture that strikingly mirrors the human bilingual lexicon and its cortical organization. With the minor theoretical refinements outlined above, this paper will become a foundational reference across NLP interpretability, psycholinguistics, and the cognitive neuroscience of language.
